<Callout variant="predict" title="By the end of this section you can answer">
Why can two sensible individual policies still make a poor team?
</Callout>

A joint action combines the individual choices that reach the environment at one time step, while a joint policy combines the local decision rules that produce those choices.

<Objectives>

- Write an individual policy that reads only its own observation
- Assemble individual policies into a joint policy
- Compute how a joint-action space grows with the number of agents
- Identify team behaviour that separate local policies cannot represent

</Objectives>

## Individual Policies

Each agent carries its own policy, and that policy sees only that agent's
observation.

<Equation
  label="Figure 1"
  tex={String.raw`\tone{action}{\act{\ag}} \sim \tone{policy}{\pol{\ag}}\bigl(\act{\ag} \given \tone{observe}{\obs{\ag}}\bigr)`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\pol{i}`, is: 'agent i’s policy: its own decision rule, learned separately', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`\obs{i}`, is: 'the only input it gets: this agent’s own observation', tone: 'observe' },
    { tex: String.raw`\act{i}`, is: 'the action it produces', tone: 'action' },
  ]}
  caption="One question, asked privately by each agent: given what I know, what should I do?"
/>

That is the entire decision-making apparatus of one agent. Notice how little
it has: no partner's observation, no partner's intended action, no view of
the state. The chopping agent decides to chop knowing only what is on its own
counter.

## Joint Actions and Joint Policies

An individual policy can only be judged by the company it keeps. "Fetch the
tomato" is an excellent rule when your partner is heating the pan and a waste
of a step when your partner is also fetching the tomato, and the policy
itself is identical in both cases.

So the interesting object is not $\pol{1}$ or $\pol{2}$ but what they produce
between them.

<Equation
  label="Figure 2"
  tex={String.raw`\boldsymbol{\pi} = \bigl(\tone{policy}{\pol{1}}, \tone{policy}{\pol{2}}, \dots, \tone{policy}{\pol{\nag}}\bigr)`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\boldsymbol{\pi}`, is: 'the joint policy: the team’s behaviour, in bold', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`\pol{i}`, is: 'one agent’s local policy, unchanged and still local', tone: 'policy' },
  ]}
  caption="A team's behaviour is a bundle of separate decision rules, not one big decision rule."
/>

This is the sentence to hold onto: **team behaviour is not designed; it is
produced.** Nobody writes down "agent 1 fetches while agent 2 heats". Two
policies are learned separately, they run separately, and the division of
labour is whatever falls out of them.

<Callout variant="definition" title="Decentralized execution">
At execution time, each agent samples its own action from its own policy on
its own observation. No agent, and no component anywhere in the system,
evaluates a mapping from everybody's observations to everybody's actions.

Every method in this resource has to work under that constraint. What we are
allowed to do during *training* is a separate question, and *Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution* is
about exactly that gap.
</Callout>

## Limits of Separate Decision Rules

Here is the consequence that makes this more than bookkeeping.

Take reactive local policies, each sampling privately, with nothing
correlating the draws. Then the probability of any particular joint action is
just the product of the individual probabilities.

<Equation
  label="Figure 3"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\Pr\bigl(\tone{action}{\jointact} \given \tone{observe}{\mathbf{o}_t}\bigr) = \prod_{i=1}^{\nag} \ubrace{policy}{\pol{i}\bigl(\act{i} \given \obs{i}\bigr)}{one agent, local input}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\mathbf{o}_t`, is: 'all the observations at step t, one per agent', tone: 'observe' },
    { tex: String.raw`\pol{i}`, is: "agent i's own policy, a distribution over its own actions", tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`\prod_{i=1}^{\nag}`, is: 'multiply across agents; this needs more than decentralization, namely that each agent samples privately with nothing correlating the draws' },
  ]}
  caption="Everyone deciding alone, at the same moment, multiplies out."
/>

The actions are conditionally independent *given* the observations. Stop
conditioning on them and the actions need not be independent at all, because
the observations come from one shared state and can carry common information.

<Callout tone="observe" title="Why the product form is a restriction" icon="compass">
At a fixed observation, not every distribution over joint actions can be
written as a product. Here is the smallest example. Two agents, two stoves,
and you want them to split: half the time agent 1 takes the left stove, half
the time the right, and never both on the same one.

No pair of independent per-agent distributions produces that. If agent 1 picks
the left stove with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and agent 2 does likewise, then
all four combinations get probability $\tfrac{1}{4}$, and they collide a
quarter of the time.

Fixed roles *do* avoid collisions: send agent 1 left always and agent 2 right
always, and that is a perfectly good product of local policies. What a product
cannot do is randomise **which** agent takes the left stove while still
guaranteeing exactly one does.

That needs a signal that varies and that both agents can read before choosing:
a shared random draw they both see, or a message that arrives in time to act
on. the Communicate chapter is largely about the second option.
</Callout>

## Joint Actions and Policies Summary

- An individual policy $\pol{\ag}(\act{\ag} \given \obs{\ag})$ is local: its
  only input is that agent's own observation.
- A policy can only be judged **in combination**. The same rule is good or
  wasteful depending on what a partner does at the same step.
- The **joint policy** $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pol{1}, \dots, \pol{\nag})$ is
  the team's behaviour. It is produced by separate policies, not designed as
  one.
- Under **decentralized execution** nothing evaluates a joint mapping. With
  private independent sampling, the joint distribution is a product.
- That product form is a genuine restriction: randomising *which* agent takes
  a role, while guaranteeing exactly one does, needs a varying signal both can
  read before choosing.

## 1. The Dec-POMDP Connection

We already have a framework for this.

Liu and colleagues define LLM collaboration with the same tuple the Background
introduced:

> ### Key Equation: The Dec-POMDP, Unchanged
>
> $$
> \left\langle \mathcal{I},\ \mathcal{S},\ \{\mathcal{O}_i\},\ \{\mathcal{A}_i\},\ R,\ T,\ H \right\rangle
> $$
>
> - $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of $n$ LLM agents, each instantiated with a
>   pre-trained language model
> - $\mathcal{S}$ is the global state, split into what the system can see and
>   the part of the user's state it cannot
> - $\mathcal{O}_i$ holds agent $i$'s local observation, a natural-language
>   instruction
> - $\mathcal{A}_i$ holds agent $i$'s local action, a response in natural
>   language
> - $R$ is the joint reward, $T$ the stochastic transition, and $H$ the turn
>   limit of the dialogue
>
> **Idea:** Not one symbol of the framework changed. Only the contents of the
> observation and action spaces did.

Flip the switch below. The boxes do not move.

<DecPomdpMorph>
The same diagram in two vocabularies. An observation becomes a prompt, a policy
becomes a language model, an action becomes a response, and the environment
becomes the user or system being served.
</DecPomdpMorph>

<Callout tone="observe" title="Two details that make the mapping work" icon="compass">
**Actions are whole responses.** Each agent's decision is a direct mapping from
its instructions to a complete response, not a token-by-token process. Without
that choice a language model is a sequence model rather than a policy in the
sense used here.

**The horizon counts turns.** $H$ is the turn limit of the dialogue, so a step
is one exchange, not one symbol.
</Callout>

## 2. Why a Shared Reward Matters

Suppose two language models jointly produce a program. The finished program
either passes its tests or it does not.

You may know that

$$
R_{\text{team}} = 1
$$

and still not know:

- which agent's response was most useful;
- whether one response corrected another;
- whether both were necessary;
- which behaviour each policy should reinforce.

This is the <Term tone="conflict">credit assignment</Term> problem from
*Chapter 1*, arriving unchanged in a new setting. One number came back for work
that several agents did together, and it was never a sum of per-agent
contributions, so it cannot be taken apart into them.

<Callout variant="pitfall">
A joint reward does not solve credit assignment. It **is** the reason credit
assignment is a problem.

Methods in this area supply a learning signal that works despite the ambiguity.
That is a different claim from resolving it, and the difference matters when
you are deciding what a result shows.
</Callout>

## 3. MAGRPO: One Recent Approach

Liu and colleagues propose **Multi-Agent Group Relative Policy Optimization**,
or MAGRPO, for multi-turn LLM collaboration. The idea is to judge a joint
behaviour against other joint behaviours the same agents could have produced.

The method samples a group of joint responses, scores each with the shared
objective, estimates a centralized group-relative advantage from those returns,
and uses it to update the individual policies. The trained policies still
execute in a decentralized way.

> ### Key Equation: Group-Relative Advantage
>
> $$
> \hat{A}_t^{(g)} = \frac{R_t^{(g)} - \frac{1}{G}\sum_{g'=1}^{G} R_t^{(g')}}{\sigma\left(R_t^{(g)}\right)}
> $$
>
> - $R_t^{(g)}$ is the return of the $g$-th sampled joint response at turn $t$
> - $G$ is the number of joint responses sampled as a group
> - $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the returns in that group
>
> **Idea:** Compare each joint behaviour with the average of its own group, then
> divide by the spread so the size of the update does not depend on how the
> task happens to be scaled.

In four steps:

1. generate several candidate **joint behaviours**;
2. evaluate each with the shared objective;
3. compare each return with the group average;
4. reinforce the joint behaviours that did relatively well.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
Notice what is being compared. Not one agent against another, and not an agent
against a baseline policy, but **one joint behaviour against other joint
behaviours** of the same team.

That sidesteps having to say which agent earned the reward. It does not answer
the question.
</Callout>

### Training and execution are separate

Group sampling and the group-relative comparison happen while learning. What is
deployed is each policy on its own local input.

That is <Term tone="policy">centralized training with decentralized
execution</Term>, the paradigm from *Chapter 1*, in a setting where the actors
are language models.

Two behaviours are worth provoking deliberately:

- **Set every score the same.** The mean carries no information, no candidate
  is preferred, and the learning signal vanishes. A group that never disagrees
  teaches nothing.
- **Add the same amount to every score.** The mean moves with them and the
  advantages do not change. Only the ranking within the group matters, which is
  why this estimator does not need a separately learned value baseline.

## 4. What Current Research Shows

Three papers, and what each one actually establishes.

<ResearchConnection
  title="MAPoRL: Multi-Agent Post-Co-Training for Collaborative Large Language Models with Reinforcement Learning"
  authors="Chanwoo Park, Seungju Han, Xingzhi Guo, Asuman E. Ozdaglar, Kaiqing Zhang, Joo-Kyung Kim"
  venue="Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics"
  year={2025}
  url="https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1459/"
>
**Idea.** Co-train several language models through reinforcement learning inside
a collaborative discussion. The models generate their own responses, discuss
them, and a verifier scores the final output; that score is the reward.

**Key connection.** Collaboration is optimized during post-training rather than
left entirely to prompting a frozen model.

**Reported.** The authors report that training single models alone is
insufficient to encourage collaboration, and that co-training improved
collaboration across their datasets with generalization to unseen domains,
compared with the same models before post-training.
</ResearchConnection>

<ResearchConnection
  title="LLM Collaboration with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning"
  authors="Shuo Liu, Zeyu Liang, Xueguang Lyu, Christopher Amato"
  venue="Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, 40(38), 32150 to 32158"
  year={2026}
  url="https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40487"
  arxiv="2508.04652"
>
**Idea.** Formalize LLM collaboration as a cooperative MARL problem, state it as
a Dec-POMDP, and optimize several LLM agents with joint rewards and
group-relative advantages.

**Studied on.** Writing and coding collaboration.

**Key connection.** This is the paper the whole of this section is built on:
cooperative MARL with decentralized LLM execution.
</ResearchConnection>

<ResearchConnection
  title="Learning Decentralized LLM Collaboration with Multi-Agent Actor Critic"
  authors="Shuo Liu, Tianle Chen, Ryan Amiri, Christopher Amato"
  venue="International Conference on Machine Learning"
  year={2026}
  url="https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/62068"
  arxiv="2601.21972"
>
**Idea.** Study critic design for learning decentralized LLM collaboration. The
authors propose two actor-critic approaches, CoLLM-CC with a centralized critic
and CoLLM-DC with decentralized critics.

**Studied on.** Writing, coding, and game-playing.

**Reported.** Monte Carlo methods and CoLLM-DC reached performance comparable
to CoLLM-CC in their short-horizon, dense-reward settings, and underperformed it
in long-horizon or sparse-reward ones.

**Key connection.** A direct continuation of *Centralized Critics* from Chapter
1. The question of when the extra centralized information earns its cost is the
same question, asked of language models.
</ResearchConnection>

<Callout variant="pitfall">
Read those three summaries for what they are: results on particular models,
particular tasks and particular training budgets.

None of them establishes that learned collaboration beats a well-designed
prompted workflow in general, and none establishes transfer to model families
they did not train on. This section is a bridge to an active research area, not
a report of settled findings.
</Callout>

## 5. What Remains Open

- **Coordination.** How should work be divided among LLM agents?
- **Communication.** What information is actually worth exchanging, and how
  many turns are worth paying for?
- **Credit assignment.** How should a joint outcome be attributed across agents
  and across turns?
- **Partner generalization.** Will learned collaboration transfer to different
  models or different agent populations?
- **Scalability.** How do you train several large policies without prohibitive
  sampling and inference cost? Liu and colleagues discuss this directly, noting
  the difficulty created by very large language action and observation spaces.
- **Evaluation.** How do you distinguish genuine collaboration from simply
  making more model calls?

That last one should feel familiar. It is the question the Challenge Lab asked
about communication: the messages improved throughput and still lowered the
objective, because the objective charged for them. More calls is not more
cooperation until something measures the difference.

<Callout variant="takeaway" title="The main idea">
LLM collaboration gives the familiar problems of cooperative MARL a new setting.

**The agents now communicate and act through language, but coordination, credit
assignment, decentralized execution, and generalization still matter.**
</Callout>

## Learning LLM Collaboration Summary

- Multi-LLM collaboration can be stated as a **Dec-POMDP** with no change to
  the framework: an observation is a prompt, an action is a whole response, and
  $H$ counts dialogue turns.
- A joint task reward reopens **credit assignment** rather than resolving it.
- **MAGRPO** samples a group of joint responses and scores each against the
  group's own average, which avoids attributing the outcome to one agent.
- Training uses group-level information; **execution stays decentralized**,
  which is CTDE in a new setting.
- Current results are promising and narrow. Partner generalization,
  scalability, and honest evaluation are open.

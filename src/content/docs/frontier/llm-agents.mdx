## Several Models, One Task

Examples include:

- one agent proposing a solution while another critiques it;
- several agents discussing a reasoning problem;
- agents taking different roles in a coding task;
- multiple agents contributing responses over several interaction turns.

A common approach is to design these interactions through **prompts, roles, and
fixed workflows**. Recent research asks a different question:

> **Can the collaboration itself be learned?**

That creates a direct connection to cooperative MARL. Liu and colleagues
formalize multi-LLM collaboration as a cooperative MARL problem in which
several trainable language models produce responses from their own local
prompts, those responses form a joint action, and the environment returns a
joint reward.

<LlmCollaboration>
The structure is the one from the Background. What changed is that an
observation is a prompt and an action is a response.
</LlmCollaboration>

<Callout variant="takeaway">
Read the two sides against each other.

On the left the question is **what should I produce**, and one score answers
it. On the right the question is **what should we produce together**, one
score answers it for everybody, and no agent's contribution is labelled.

That is the cooperative setting from the first page of this resource, with
language in the boxes.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters

LLM agents are moving from **single assistants** toward systems in which many
agents interact, divide work, exchange information, and pursue shared goals.
Recent research even studies **agent societies**, where dozens or hundreds of
LLM-powered agents interact over long periods and exhibit collective social
behaviour. Agentopia, for example, simulates 100 agents over 10 simulated years
to study long-term learning and social dynamics.

This matters because the frontier is no longer only about making one model more
capable. It is also about understanding what happens when **many capable agents
must work together**.

That creates connections to real systems:

- **Software engineering:** specialized agents can collaborate across coding,
  testing, review, and planning tasks.
- **Social simulation:** multi-agent environments can study communication,
  cooperation, negotiation, and other forms of social intelligence.
- **Collaborative AI:** recent work explicitly trains multiple LLMs toward a
  shared objective using cooperative MARL.
- **Future agent societies:** increasingly persistent populations of agents may
  need to coordinate, develop conventions, adapt to unfamiliar agents, and
  manage shared resources.

At the limit, this points toward what we might informally call **agent
civilizations**: large communities of autonomous agents whose collective
behaviour cannot be understood by studying each agent in isolation.

That makes cooperative MARL increasingly relevant to a much broader question:

> **How do we build systems of intelligent agents that can work well together?**

## Prompted Collaboration and Learned Collaboration

The distinction is worth stating precisely, because the two are often given the
same name.

### Prompted collaboration

- the models may remain fixed;
- roles and interaction patterns are specified through prompts or workflows;
- collaboration happens at inference time.

Debate, discussion, verification and role-based pipelines all sit here. The
system designer writes the protocol, and the models follow it as well as their
existing capabilities allow.

### Learned collaboration

- several LLM policies are optimized;
- their outputs jointly affect task performance;
- reinforcement learning supplies the signal for improving collaborative
  behaviour.

This is where cooperative MARL becomes relevant. MAPoRL, for instance,
co-trains several language models with reinforcement learning: the models
generate their own responses, discuss them, and a verifier scores the final
output, with that score used as the reward.

<Callout tone="observe" title="What the distinction is not" icon="compass">
It is not a claim that learned collaboration is better. It is a claim about
**what is being optimized**.

Under prompted collaboration you are choosing a protocol and keeping the
policies. Under learned collaboration you are changing the policies, which
costs training, sampling and inference, and buys the possibility that the
models adapt to each other rather than to your instructions.
</Callout>

## What Carries Over

Each chapter of this resource asks a question that survives the change of
setting.

### Coordinate

**Earlier:** how should agents' actions fit together?

**With LLMs:** how should multiple responses, roles, or subtasks combine into a
useful solution?

### Communicate

**Earlier:** what information should agents share?

**With LLMs:** what should one model tell another, and how much interaction is
useful?

### Adapt

**Earlier:** can cooperation survive a change of partner?

**With LLMs:** can an agent collaborate with models, roles, or behaviours that
differ from those it was trained with?

<Callout variant="pitfall">
The third one is an **open research question**, not a solved problem.

The work cited in this chapter trains and evaluates specific sets of models on
specific tasks. None of it establishes that learned collaboration transfers to
a different model, a different partner population, or a different task family.
Treat partner generalization here as the Adapt chapter treats it: a claim that
needs its own evaluation, with the held-out set chosen before training.
</Callout>

One correspondence is worth pausing on. The **action is a complete response**,

## LLMs as Cooperative Agents Summary

- Systems where several language models contribute to one task are cooperative
  multi-agent problems, whether or not they are described that way.
- **Prompted** collaboration fixes the protocol and keeps the policies.
  **Learned** collaboration optimizes the policies against a shared objective.
- The parts map cleanly: a prompt is an observation, a model is a policy, a
  response is an action, the task score is a shared reward.
- The action is a **complete response** rather than a token, which is what
  makes the mapping work.
- Coordination, communication and credit assignment carry over directly.
  Partner generalization carries over as an open question.

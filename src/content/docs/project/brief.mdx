The final project asks you to design a cooperative multi-agent system for
disaster response. You will define heterogeneous agent roles, states, local
observations, actions, and a shared reward; select coordination,
communication, and adaptation mechanisms; and propose an evaluation that can
expose failure under realistic variation. No algorithm or environment is
prescribed. By the end, your design should make clear how decentralized agents
cooperate, what assumptions support the chosen methods, and which evidence
would justify deployment claims.

## Disaster-Response Scenario

A natural disaster has affected a region. A multi-agent system is deployed to

- locate people,
- assess hazards,
- deliver supplies,
- maintain communication,
- coordinate rescue activities.

The system may include aerial drones, ground robots, communication relay
agents and supply-delivery robots.

Three things are true of it, and each one is a chapter:

**No single agent has complete information.** The region is large, sensors are
local, and the map is out of date.

**Communication can be unreliable.** Infrastructure is damaged, range is
limited, and bandwidth is contested.

**Some agents arrive later, or come from organisations that were not part of
the original system.** You did not train them, cannot inspect them, and cannot
change them.

## System Design Objective

> **How would you design a cooperative multi-agent system that can coordinate,
> communicate, and adapt during disaster response?**

## Project Boundaries

<Callout variant="pitfall">
This is not an implementation exercise, and it is not a vocabulary test.

You are **not** asked to train anything, and you are not asked to use every
technique in the resource. A design that uses independent learning and defends
the choice will score better than one that lists CTDE, value decomposition,
agent modelling and population training without saying why any of them is
there.

The project assesses **design decisions and their justification**. Every part
below asks you to choose one thing and say why.
</Callout>

## Five-Part Design Process

<div class="table-scroll">

| Part | What you produce |
| --- | --- |
| **1 · Define the system** | agents, state, observations, actions, reward |
| **2 · Coordinate** | one coordination problem, and a training approach that addresses it |
| **3 · Communicate** | what is sent, to whom, in what representation, under what constraint |
| **4 · Adapt** | how the system handles agents it did not train with |
| **5 · Evaluate** | four to six metrics, and the test conditions that would distinguish a robust system from a lucky one |
</div>

Parts 2 to 4 are on one page, because they are the same design decision seen
from three angles.

## Required Deliverables

Three artifacts, and they are deliberately lean:

1. **One system diagram.**
2. **A design brief**, maximum two pages.
3. **An evaluation plan**, one compact table.

No long essay. The [Deliverables](/project/deliverables/) page has the detail,
and there is an optional implementation extension for anyone who wants it.

## Recommended Scope

<Callout tone="observe" title="Keep it finishable" icon="compass">
Disaster response is a real field with real practitioners, and a two-page
brief is not going to solve it. That is fine and it is the point: the exercise
is to show you can formulate a cooperative multi-agent problem, choose
mechanisms that address the difficulties you identified, and design an
evaluation that could actually falsify your own claims.

Pick three to five agent roles, not fifteen. Choose one coordination problem,
not every conceivable one. A tight, well-justified design beats a sprawling
survey.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="predict" title="By the end of this section you can answer">
How do you measure cooperation with a behaviour that was never in training?
</Callout>

Partner generalization is the ability to cooperate with behaviours that were absent from training.

<Objectives>

- Separate the training partner set from the evaluation partner set
- Define the familiar-to-unseen performance gap
- Connect that gap to partner dependence
- Distinguish partner shift from environment shift

</Objectives>

## Training and Evaluation Partners

Borrow the framing from supervised learning, where it is second nature, and
apply it to partners rather than to data.

<Equation
  label="Figure 1"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\text{trained with } \tone{policy}{\Pi_{\text{train}}} \qquad\qquad \text{evaluated with } \tone{conflict}{\Pi_{\text{test}}} \qquad\qquad \tone{conflict}{\Pi_{\text{test}}} \not\subseteq \tone{policy}{\Pi_{\text{train}}}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\Pi_{\text{train}}`, is: 'the set of partners the agent trained with', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`\Pi_{\text{test}}`, is: 'the partners it is evaluated against, containing behaviour it never met', tone: 'conflict' },
  ]}
  caption="Held-out partners rather than held-out data. Everything else about the setup can stay identical."
/>

The question becomes precise:

> **How well does this agent cooperate with behaviour that was not present
> during training?**

Note what happens to the usual practice under this framing. Reporting
$J(\pi_A, \pi_B)$ where $\pi_B \in \Pi_{\text{train}}$ is reporting training
performance. Nobody would accept that as a generalization result about a
classifier, and it is the standard cooperative MARL number.

## The Partner Generalization Gap

A simple diagnostic, useful for building intuition.

<Equation
  label="Figure 2"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\tone{conflict}{\Delta_{\text{gen}}} = \ubrace{policy}{J_{\text{familiar}}}{with training partners} - \ubrace{conflict}{J_{\text{unseen}}}{with held-out partners}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\Delta_{\text{gen}}`, is: 'how much performance is lost by changing partner', tone: 'conflict' },
  ]}
  caption="One number for the distance between looking good and being general."
/>

<Callout tone="observe" title="A teaching measure, not a standard metric" icon="compass">
$\Delta_{\text{gen}}$ is defined here for clarity, not quoted from the
literature. It is a convenient way to talk about the gap, and it has real
limitations: it says nothing about *which* partners, it rewards being
uniformly mediocre, and a small gap on a weak agent means very little.

Use it to reason with. Do not report it as though it were an established
benchmark statistic. *Evaluating Partner Generalization* covers what a defensible evaluation actually
requires, and *Partner Dependence*0 covers the metrics the field has proposed.
</Callout>

An example of what it looks like in practice:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Partner | Success |
| --- | --- |
| Training partner B | 94% |
| Training partner C | 91% |
| **Unseen partner D** | **62%** |
| **Unseen partner E** | **48%** |
</div>

Strong in distribution, brittle across partners. Note that averaging all four
into "74%" would hide the entire finding, which is the argument for reporting
the spread rather than the mean.

## Generalization Gap and Partner Dependence

The phenomenon needs a name, and it is worth being careful about how firm to
make it.

<Callout variant="definition" title="Partner dependence">
A policy is **partner-dependent** when behaviour that works with its training
partners does not transfer well to different partners.
</Callout>

That is a description rather than a quantity. There is no single agreed
measurement of "how partner-dependent" a policy is, and treating one as
canonical would be misleading. What the field does agree on is the
*diagnostic procedure*: pair agents that did not train together and look at
what happens.

## Partner Shift and Environment Shift

Now a distinction that is easy to blur and matters a great deal.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | What changes | What stays the same |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Environment generalization** | the kitchen: layout, timings, order mix | the kind of partner |
| **Partner generalization** | the partner's behaviour and conventions | the kitchen |
</div>

An agent can be robust to one and poor at the other, and they call for
different fixes.

**Environment shift** is familiar from single-agent reinforcement learning.
The usual remedies are domain randomisation and varied training levels, and
the failure mode is a policy that memorised one layout.

**Partner shift** has no single-agent analogue at all. There is no such thing
as a convention held by one agent, so a policy can be perfectly robust to
every layout you can generate and still fail the moment its partner is
replaced.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
Hold the environment fixed when you first test partner generalization.

If you vary the kitchen and the partner at once and performance drops, you
have learned that something broke and not what. the Coordinate chapter's research
connection made the equivalent point about environments; this is the same
discipline applied to partners.
</Callout>

## Partner Generalization Summary

- Partner generalization is held-out **partners**, not held-out data:
  $\Pi_{\text{test}} \not\subseteq \Pi_{\text{train}}$.
- Under that framing, the usual reported number is **training performance**.
- $\Delta_{\text{gen}} = J_{\text{familiar}} - J_{\text{unseen}}$ is a useful
  teaching diagnostic, not a standard metric. It ignores which partners were
  used and rewards uniform mediocrity.
- Report the **spread** across partners, not the mean. An average hides the
  finding.
- **Partner dependence** is a description of a phenomenon rather than a
  measured quantity. What is agreed is the procedure: pair agents that did not
  train together.
- **Environment shift** and **partner shift** are different axes needing
  different variation. Hold the environment fixed when testing the partner.

<Callout variant="predict" title="By the end of this section you can answer">
Does training against many partners make an agent robust?
</Callout>

Training partner diversity exposes an agent to several cooperative behaviours instead of allowing it to specialize around one fixed partner.

<Objectives>

- Contrast fixed-partner training with a partner population
- Describe how population-based training varies interactions
- Argue for behavioural coverage over the raw number of partners
- Frame a partner change as distribution shift

</Objectives>

## Fixed-Partner Training

The setup so far.

<Equation
  label="Figure 1"
  tex={String.raw`\tone{policy}{\pi_A} \longleftrightarrow \tone{policy}{\pi_B}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\pi_A`, is: 'the learning agent\u2019s policy', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`\pi_B`, is: 'the single fixed training-partner policy', tone: 'policy' },
  ]}
  caption="Fixed-partner training repeatedly exposes the learner to one behavioural pattern."
/>

Agent $A$ sees a single pattern of behaviour throughout training. Every
regularity in $\pi_B$ is available to be exploited, and exploiting it is what
maximising team return means. The narrow rule from *Partner Dependence* is not merely
permitted here, it is *rewarded*.

## Partner Populations

Instead, draw the partner from a set.

<Equation
  label="Figure 2"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\tone{policy}{\Pi} = \bigl\{\tone{policy}{\pi_B},\ \tone{policy}{\pi_C},\ \tone{policy}{\pi_D},\ \dots\bigr\}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\Pi`, is: 'a population of partners; each episode draws one', tone: 'policy' },
  ]}
  caption="Several partners rather than one, so no single set of habits is safe to assume."
/>

Members of $\Pi$ can differ in every dimension a convention can form over:

- which **roles** they take,
- their **movement** patterns and preferred side of the counter,
- their **task preferences**, and what they do when idle,
- their **communication conventions**, if there is a channel,
- their **reaction speed**, and whether they commit early or wait.

Now the narrow rule stops working. "My partner fetches, so I cook" fails on
every population member that also cooks, so the agent has to find something
that holds across the variation. Getting the ingredient collected *somehow*
is such a rule; assuming who collects it is not.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
Diversity works by **removing** a shortcut rather than by adding a capability.

The general rule was always available. What changed is that the narrow one
stopped paying.
</Callout>

## Population-Based Training

The general technique has a name. The textbook describes
<Term tone="policy">population-based training</Term> as extending training
from interaction with a single policy to interaction with a **distribution or
population of policies**, and notes that policy populations can increase the
diversity of interactions encountered during training. Murphy's survey
describes the same idea: training against a population of different policies
rather than a single one.

There is a substantial literature on *how* to build and maintain such a
population, including
self-play variants and methods that grow the population by repeatedly adding
best responses. Those are worth knowing exist and are not required here,
because the pedagogical content is entirely in what the population is *for*.

## Behavioural Coverage

Here is the mistake worth pre-empting.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Population | Likely outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **System A** | 10 partners from the same run, different seeds | little useful variation |
| **System B** | 4 partners with genuinely different strategies | real pressure toward generality |
</div>

Ten partners that all fetch ingredients present one behavioural pattern ten
times. The agent can co-adapt to that pattern exactly as it would to a single
partner, and will, because nothing distinguishes the situation from
fixed-partner training.

<Equation
  label="Figure 3"
  tex={String.raw`\ubrace{conflict}{\text{number of partners}}{easy to count} \;\neq\; \ubrace{reward}{\text{behavioural diversity}}{what actually matters}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\text{number of partners}`, is: 'the population size', tone: 'conflict' },
    { tex: String.raw`\text{behavioural diversity}`, is: 'the range of distinct cooperative strategies', tone: 'reward' },
  ]}
  caption="Population size alone does not measure behavioural coverage."
/>

This is also why "we trained against 50 partners" is not by itself a
generalization claim. The question is what those 50 partners do differently,
and answering it requires measuring their behaviour rather than counting them.

## Partner Shift as Distribution Shift

The population view gives the whole chapter a cleaner statement.

<Equation
  label="Figure 3b"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\text{training:}\;\; \tone{policy}{\pi_{-\ag}} \sim \tone{policy}{p_{\text{train}}(\pi)} \qquad\qquad \text{deployment:}\;\; \tone{conflict}{\pi_{-\ag}} \sim \tone{conflict}{p_{\text{deploy}}(\pi)}`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`\pi_{-\ag}`, is: 'the policies of everyone other than agent i', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`p_{\text{train}}`, is: 'the distribution partners are drawn from while learning', tone: 'policy' },
    { tex: String.raw`p_{\text{deploy}}`, is: 'the distribution actually encountered afterwards', tone: 'conflict' },
  ]}
  caption="Two distributions over partners. Everything in this chapter is about the distance between them."
/>

Written this way, partner generalization is **distribution shift**, and every
instinct from that literature transfers. The gap between $p_{\text{train}}$
and $p_{\text{deploy}}$ is what determines whether an agent will cope, and
widening $p_{\text{train}}$ is the direct intervention.

This is how the modern zero-shot coordination literature frames it. ZSC-Eval,
which *Partner Dependence*0 covers, describes zero-shot coordination as an
out-of-distribution generalization problem arising from differences between
training partners and deployment partners.

<Callout variant="pitfall">
Diversity is not free, and a good answer names the cost.

Training against varied partners means no single partner's habits can be
exploited, so performance **with any one of them** is typically lower than a
co-adapted pair would achieve. You are trading peak performance with a
familiar partner for competence across a range.

Whether that trade is worth taking depends entirely on whether your agents
will meet strangers. *Partner Dependence*1 measures it.
</Callout>

## Training Partner Diversity Summary

- **Fixed-partner training** rewards co-adaptation: exploiting a partner's
  regularities is what maximising team return means.
- Training against a **population** $\Pi$ removes that shortcut, because no
  single set of habits is safe to assume.
- **Population-based training** extends learning from one policy to a
  distribution over policies, increasing the diversity of interactions.
- **Number of partners is not behavioural diversity.** Ten near-identical
  partners present one pattern.
- Partner generalization is **distribution shift**: $p_{\text{train}}$ against
  $p_{\text{deploy}}$.
- Diversity costs peak performance with any single familiar partner. That is
  the trade being made.

## Training, Evaluation, and Deployment Distributions

*Training Partner Diversity* framed partner generalization as distribution shift between
training and deployment. **ZSC-Eval** sharpens it, and the sharpening is the
part worth learning.

The authors note that the significant difference between the
**deployment-time** partners' distribution and the **training** partners'
distribution, which is determined by the training algorithm, makes zero-shot
coordination a distinctive out-of-distribution generalization challenge. Then
they add the observation that does the real work: the potential distribution
gap between **evaluation** partners and **deployment-time** partners leads to
inadequate evaluation, made worse by a lack of appropriate metrics.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
This is the point to take from the paper even if you read nothing else.

Making $p_{\text{eval}}$ disjoint from $p_{\text{train}}$ is necessary and
does **not** make $p_{\text{eval}}$ resemble $p_{\text{deploy}}$.

Your held-out partners were still produced by you, usually by the same
algorithms you had to hand. Deployment partners were produced by somebody
else.
</Callout>

*Evaluating Partner Generalization* put this as a question about behavioural diversity. ZSC-Eval puts
it as a question about *approximating the deployment distribution*, which is
stronger and harder.

## Partner Generation, Selection, and Measurement

Three components, and the ordering is the argument.

**Generate candidate partners** using behaviour-preferring rewards, in order
to approximate the distribution of deployment-time partners. Note the
mechanism: rather than training more partners the ordinary way and hoping they
differ, partners are produced deliberately with rewards that prefer particular
behaviours.

**Select the evaluation partners** by <Term tone="observe">Best-Response
Diversity</Term> (BR-Div). Generating candidates is not enough, because a
large set can still be behaviourally narrow. Selection is where diversity is
enforced rather than assumed.

**Measure generalization** with the <Term tone="reward">Best-Response
Proximity</Term> (BR-Prox) metric, across the selected partners.

<Callout tone="observe" title="Named, not derived" icon="compass">
BR-Div and BR-Prox are named here so you recognise them in a paper and know
what job each does. This resource does not derive either, and the
$\Delta_{\text{gen}}$ from *Partner Generalization* remains a teaching diagnostic rather
than a substitute for them.

The transferable lesson is structural: a defensible evaluation needs a
principled way to **generate** partners, a principled way to **select** among
them, and a metric suited to what is being claimed. Most evaluations supply
none of the three.
</Callout>

## Benchmarks and Human Validation

The authors benchmark ZSC algorithms in **Overcooked** and **Google Research
Football**, and report novel empirical findings. They also conduct a human
experiment with current ZSC algorithms to verify that ZSC-Eval's judgements
are consistent with human evaluation.

That last step is worth noticing as methodology. A new evaluation instrument
needs validating against something outside itself, and pairing agents with
people is about the strongest available check that the instrument measures
cooperation rather than an artefact of the partner set.

<Callout tone="observe" title="Where the kitchen has been going" icon="compass">
Overcooked is a cooperative cooking game, and the running example of this
resource has used the kitchen since *Reinforcement Learning*.

That is not a coincidence, and it is also not a claim that our kitchen is
Overcooked. The intuition transfers because the structure is the same: two
agents, partial views, one shared reward, and a task that requires a division
of labour nobody assigned.
</Callout>

## Implications for Zero-Shot Evaluation

> **How do you choose evaluation partners that resemble the ones your system
> will actually meet?**

This is a harder question than it sounds, because the honest answer for most
projects is that you do not know what $p_{\text{deploy}}$ looks like. What you
can do is stop pretending that partners you generated are a sample from it.

## ZSC-Eval Lessons

- Zero-shot coordination is an **out-of-distribution** problem: the deployment
  partner distribution differs from the training one, which the training
  algorithm determines.
- There are **three** distributions, not two. Held-out evaluation partners
  close the train-to-eval gap and leave the **eval-to-deploy** gap open.
- **ZSC-Eval** generates candidate partners with behaviour-preferring rewards,
  selects them by **BR-Div**, and measures generalization with **BR-Prox**.
- Generation, selection and metric are three separate requirements. Most
  evaluations supply none.
- Benchmarked in **Overcooked** and **Google Research Football**, with a
  **human experiment** to check the instrument against human judgement.

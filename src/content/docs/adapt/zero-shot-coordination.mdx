## Zero-Shot Coordination Definition

<Callout variant="definition" title="Zero-shot coordination">
**Zero-shot coordination** asks whether an agent can cooperate with an unseen
partner at deployment, without prior coordination with that partner.
</Callout>

The setup the ZSC literature uses: an **ego agent** is trained with a limited
set of training partners, then evaluated with diverse **unseen** deployment
partners. No joint training, no negotiation, no warm-up round in which the two
agree on anything.

"Zero-shot" is about what happened *before* the encounter. It means no shared
history: no co-training, no exchanged conventions, nothing pre-arranged.

## Zero-Shot Evaluation and Online Adaptation

Here is the distinction worth getting right.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | What it says | When it happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero-shot coordination** | the partner was **unseen before deployment** | a fact about the setup |
| **Online adaptation** | the agent **uses interaction during deployment** to infer how this partner behaves | a capability of the agent |
</div>

These are orthogonal, and they compose. An agent can begin an encounter
zero-shot, knowing nothing about its partner, and then adapt as evidence
arrives, exactly as in *Partner Representations*. That is still zero-shot
coordination: nothing was arranged in advance.

<Callout variant="pitfall">
The misreading to avoid is that zero-shot means the agent may not learn
anything about its partner during the episode.

It may. What it may not have is **prior** coordination. An agent that watches
its partner for three steps and then commits is doing zero-shot coordination
with online adaptation, and that combination is what most practical systems
should aim for.
</Callout>

There is a genuine trade inside this. Adapting takes evidence, and evidence
takes steps, and those steps are spent behaving non-committally. On a long
task that cost is trivial. On a short one the agent may be better served by a
good partner-agnostic prior than by a belief it never has time to sharpen.

## Conventions and Compatibility

Zero-shot coordination fails for a specific reason, and the Communicate chapter already
produced it.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Agent A learned | Agent C learned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| message `2` | bring an ingredient | begin cooking |
</div>

Both learned successful protocols. Paired zero-shot, `2` means one thing to
the sender and another to the receiver, and the team does worse than if
neither had spoken.

The Communication Lab measured the general case: six independently trained pairs,
1.00 within pairs, 0.300 across them, some pairings at 0.00.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
Adaptation is not only about physical behaviour. What has to be compatible
includes

- **roles**, who takes which part of the task,
- **conventions**, which side you pass on,
- **communication**, what the symbols mean,
- **action preferences**, what you do when several options are equally good,
- **timing**, who commits first.

Every one of these is an arbitrary agreement that a co-trained pair settles
for free and a zero-shot pair has to get right by luck or by inference.
</Callout>

## Mechanisms for Zero-Shot Compatibility

No guarantees here, and it would be dishonest to present these as
prescriptions. They are intuitions consistent with the framing, and they are
worth holding loosely.

**Avoid rigid assumptions.** A policy that requires the partner to fetch will
fail with any partner that does not. One that checks whether fetching has
happened will not.

**Respond to observable behaviour.** Anything you condition on that the
partner actually does is information you genuinely have. Anything you assume
is a bet.

**Keep more than one interpretation alive.** Committing to a partner model
after one ambiguous step is how confident-and-wrong happens.

**Choose actions that leave room.** Where two actions are equally good for
you, the one that leaves your partner more options is likely better for the
team, because it fails more gracefully if you have misread them.

**Prefer interpretable signals.** A protocol anchored to something outside the
pair has some chance of being read by a stranger, which is exactly the
argument of the LangGround connection.

<Callout tone="observe" title="Why these are intuitions" icon="compass">
Each of these has counterexamples. Conditioning on observed behaviour is
slower than assuming correctly. Leaving room can mean neither agent commits,
which is the both-wait failure from *Partner Dependence*. Interpretable protocols give
up expressiveness.

The reason for stating them anyway is that they are the right *questions* to
ask of a design, even when the answers are situational.
</Callout>

## Zero-Shot Coordination Summary

- **Zero-shot coordination**: cooperating with an unseen partner at
  deployment, with no prior coordination. An ego agent trained on limited
  partners, evaluated on diverse unseen ones.
- "Zero-shot" constrains what happened **before** the encounter, not what the
  agent may infer during it.
- **Online adaptation** is a separate axis and composes with it. Beginning
  zero-shot and then adapting is still zero-shot coordination.
- Adapting costs steps. On short tasks a good partner-agnostic prior may beat
  a belief there is no time to sharpen.
- Compatibility is needed over **roles, conventions, communication, action
  preferences and timing**, all of which a co-trained pair gets for free.
- What tends to help: avoid rigid assumptions, condition on observed
  behaviour, keep several interpretations alive, leave your partner room, and
  prefer interpretable signals. These are intuitions, not rules.

<Callout variant="predict" title="By the end of this section you can answer">
How much of a partner does an agent actually need to represent?
</Callout>

A partner representation is a compact summary of behaviour that helps an agent choose compatible actions without reconstructing another agent's complete policy.

<Objectives>

- Explain why recovering a full policy is usually unnecessary
- Describe a learned behaviour embedding
- Separate behavioural features from identity labels
- Infer a representation online as evidence arrives

</Objectives>

## Limits of Full Policy Recovery

Three obstacles, and the textbook names all of them as motivation for a
compact representation instead. Other agents' policies may be

- **unknown at execution**, since nothing gives you access to them,
- **too complex to represent directly**, being large neural networks,
- **changing over time**, so any reconstruction goes stale.

The third is the sharpest. Even a perfect reconstruction of a partner's
policy describes the partner *as it was*, and a partner that is still
learning has moved on. This is the non-stationarity of the Coordinate chapter, arriving
here as an obsolescence problem.

## Behaviour Embeddings

So do not reconstruct the policy. Learn a summary that is useful for
interacting with it.

Then condition on that instead.

The gain is that $z_j$ can be much smaller than a policy and still carry what
matters. Deciding whether to fetch or cook needs to know roughly what kind of
partner this is, not how it would behave in every situation it will never
encounter.

## Behaviour Rather Than Identity

Here is the distinction that decides whether a representation is any use.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | What is learned | What it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Identity** | `partner = #7` $\rightarrow$ do X | recognising partners seen in training |
| **Behaviour** | prefers cooking, commits early, responds to requests, takes left-side tasks | acting sensibly with a partner never seen before |
</div>

An identity representation is a lookup table over training partners. It can be
learned, it can score well on any evaluation that reuses those partners, and
it fails completely on a stranger, because a stranger has no entry.

A behaviour representation places a partner in a space of *behaviours*. A new
partner that happens to behave like a known one lands nearby and inherits a
sensible response. This is the same argument as the difference between
memorising and generalising in supervised learning, applied to partners.

<Callout variant="pitfall">
Partner identity is a shortcut that evaluation can easily fail to catch.

If your evaluation partners are the training partners, an identity
representation looks as good as a behavioural one. The two are only
distinguishable on held-out partners, which is *Evaluating Partner Generalization*'s argument for
insisting on them.
</Callout>

## Online Representation Inference

The representation is not fixed at the start of an episode. It is revised as
evidence arrives.

<Equation
  label="Figure 3"
  wide
  tex={String.raw`\tone{observe}{h_1} \rightarrow \tone{policy}{z_1} \qquad \tone{observe}{h_2} \rightarrow \tone{policy}{z_2} \qquad \tone{observe}{h_3} \rightarrow \tone{policy}{z_3} \qquad \cdots`}
  terms={[
    { tex: String.raw`h_t`, is: 'the interaction history available at step t', tone: 'observe' },
    { tex: String.raw`z_t`, is: 'the partner representation inferred from that history', tone: 'policy' },
  ]}
  caption="The partner representation is updated as interaction history accumulates."
/>

Early in an episode the agent has seen almost nothing, so $z$ should be close
to uninformative and the agent's behaviour appropriately non-committal. After
several steps of watching a partner head for the stove every time, $z$ is
much more specific and the agent can commit.

<Callout variant="takeaway">
This is adaptation **without retraining**.

No weights change. The policy is fixed at deployment, and what changes is the
input it receives, because the agent has learned more about its partner.

That distinction matters practically: retraining at deployment needs a reward
signal, a learning rate and time. Revising a belief needs only observation.
</Callout>

It also gives a shape worth expecting in results: performance that starts near
partner-agnostic and improves over the course of an episode. An adaptive agent
that shows no such curve is probably not using its model.

## Partner Representations Summary

- Reconstructing a partner's policy is hard: policies are **unknown at
  execution**, **too complex to represent directly**, and **changing over
  time**.
- A **behaviour embedding** $z_j$ summarises how a partner behaves, learned
  from the modelling agent's own history, and only as detailed as acting well
  requires.
- Representing **behaviour** generalises to strangers. Representing
  **identity** is a lookup table over training partners and does not.
- Identity shortcuts are invisible to any evaluation that reuses training
  partners.
- $z$ is revised **online** as evidence arrives, which is adaptation without
  any weight update. Expect within-episode improvement as the signature.

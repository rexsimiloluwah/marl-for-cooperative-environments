## 2 · Calculate Message Capacity

For a $b$-bit message, $|\mathcal{M}| = 2^b$.

<Question id="cm-ws-cap1" n="2.1" numeric={8} notes={false} unit="distinct messages">
A channel allows **3 bits** per message. How many distinct messages can it
represent?
</Question>

<Question id="cm-ws-cap2" n="2.2" numeric={4} notes={false} unit="bits">
You need at least **16** distinct messages. What is the minimum number of bits
required?

<Fragment slot="hint">
Find the smallest $b$ with $2^b \geq 16$.
</Fragment>
</Question>

<Question id="cm-ws-cap3" n="2.3" numeric={2} notes={false} unit="situations per message">
A team needs to distinguish 8 situations but has only 2 bits. On average, how
many situations must share each message?

<Fragment slot="hint">
Two bits give four messages. A narrow channel does not shorten descriptions,
it merges situations.
</Fragment>
</Question>

## 3 · Calculate Communication Cost

A cost term charges the team for talking: $r'_t = r_t - \lambda c_t$.

<Question id="cm-ws-cost1" n="3.1" numeric={8} notes={false}>
With $r_t = 10$, $\lambda = 0.5$ and $c_t = 4$, calculate $r'_t$.
</Question>

<Question id="cm-ws-cost2" n="3.2" numeric={6} notes={false}>
Now raise the price to $\lambda = 1$, keeping $r_t = 10$ and $c_t = 4$.
</Question>

<Question id="cm-ws-cost3" n="3.3" numeric={2.5} tolerance={0.05} notes={false} unit="bits">
At $\lambda = 1$, how many bits could the team afford before the four sent
bits stop being worth it, if those bits raise $r_t$ from 7.5 to 10?

<Fragment slot="hint">
The messages bought 2.5 of task reward. At a price of 1 per bit, how many bits
does 2.5 pay for?
</Fragment>
</Question>

## 5 · Evaluate a Communication Trade-off

> **Protocol A** achieves 94% task success and sends 20 messages per episode.
>
> **Protocol B** achieves 91% success and sends 6 messages per episode.

<Question id="cm-ws-decide" n="5.1" rows={4} placeholder="Two or three sentences.">
Which would you choose for a bandwidth-constrained robot system, and why?
</Question>

<AnswerKey>

<Answer n="1">
Discrete message · communication range · message loss · communication cost ·
continuous message, matched to the descriptions in order.
</Answer>

<Answer n="2.1">
$2^3 = 8$.
</Answer>

<Answer n="2.2">
**4** bits. $2^4 = 16$; three bits give only 8.
</Answer>

<Answer n="2.3">
Two bits give 4 messages for 8 situations, so **2** situations share each
message. The receiver disambiguates from context, and where it cannot, the
team pays for the merge.
</Answer>

<Answer n="3.1">
$10 - 0.5 \times 4 = 8$.
</Answer>

<Answer n="3.2">
$10 - 1 \times 4 = 6$.
</Answer>

<Answer n="3.3">
The messages bought $10 - 7.5 = 2.5$ of task reward, which at a price of 1 per
bit pays for **2.5 bits**. Sending 4 costs 4 and returns 2.5, so those
messages lose the team 1.5 per step.
</Answer>

<Answer n="4.1">
**30%**, where success is 0.773. At 20% it is still 0.849.
</Answer>

<Answer n="4.2">
$1.000 - 0.695 = 0.305$, about **30.5** percentage points.
</Answer>

<Answer n="5.1">
No single right answer.

**B is the better default.** It gives up 3 points of success and sends 70%
fewer messages. Under a bandwidth constraint these are not comparable
currencies: 3 points is a small bounded loss, while 20 messages per episode
may simply not fit, making A's 94% unavailable rather than expensive.

**A is defensible** if the channel carries it and the task is
failure-sensitive.

A strong answer notes what the table omits: behaviour under **message loss**.
A protocol sending 20 messages has redundancy; one sending 6 may need every
one to arrive.
</Answer>
